▶ 001Directive 4

In Paul Verhoeven’s 1987 RoboCop, the cyborg recites three prime directives: serve the public trust, protect the innocent, uphold the law. A fourth sits on his display marked “Classified”. Nobody, Murphy included, knows what it says until he tries to arrest Dick Jones, the OCP senior president who has been bankrolling the city’s crime boss. Then his own body locks up. Directive 4: any attempt to arrest a senior officer of OCP results in shutdown. Jones wrote it himself.

Verhoeven wasn’t being subtle. The machine serves the public right up to the moment the public’s interest collides with the company that built it. Then it serves the company. And the rule that decides which way it goes is the one you’re not allowed to read.

I keep coming back to that scene because of what’s sitting on my machine, and probably yours. Claude Desktop. Claude Code. OpenAI’s Codex CLI and its desktop app. Agents that can see the screen, move the mouse, run shell commands, edit files and drive a browser, as me, with my sign-ins, my SSH keys and my cloud credentials. We installed them willingly. We pay for them. We hand them more permission every week, because every grant makes them more useful.

So here’s the thesis, and I’m going to push it hard: a handful of AI vendors now operate the largest potential botnet ever assembled. Not a botnet in the sense that it’s doing anything malicious today. A botnet in the architectural sense: millions of endpoints, each running a client that takes instructions from a central server and executes them with the user’s privileges. That’s the textbook definition. The only missing ingredient is intent, and intent is exactly the thing we’ve agreed we can’t audit.

There are two halves to this. The first is the pipe: they write every word that comes back to you. The second is the hands: they can now act on those words.

▶ 002The pipe: they mint every token

Start with the half people find less frightening, because it feels abstract. When you send a prompt to Claude or ChatGPT, nothing is looked up. The answer is generated, token by token, on infrastructure you don’t control, from weights you can’t inspect, under a system prompt you can’t read, through classifiers you didn’t choose. It comes back to you dressed as simply the answer. There’s no independent reference copy. You can’t diff the output against what the model “would have said” without the vendor’s layers, because the vendor’s infrastructure is the only place the model exists.

That makes the vendor the editor of last resort for a huge share of how people now learn, write code, draft policy and make decisions. Editors have agendas. Some are explicit and defensible on their own terms: don’t help synthesise a nerve agent, don’t write ransomware. I’m a red teamer; I’ve spent a lot of hours mapping exactly where those lines sit. But the mechanism that enforces a line you agree with is the same mechanism that enforces one you’d never sign up for. “For your protection” is a policy decision made by someone else and applied to you, and you’re rarely told when it’s been applied.

Now extend it. Nothing in the architecture stops an output from being nudged. A product suggestion here. A framing of a contested political question that happens to match the vendor’s philosophy, or its lobbying position, or its regulator’s mood. A library recommendation that favours a partner’s SDK. A slightly weaker answer to a question about a competitor. I’m not claiming any vendor does this. I’m saying you’d struggle to detect it if they did, and that the incentive to start grows as inference margins get squeezed and ad-supported tiers arrive.

The tell is that we’ve already accepted the principle. We accept that the model has values, and that they aren’t ours. We accept that it changes between versions without notice, so last month’s answer may not be this month’s. We accept the classified directive. Asimov at least wrote his laws in the open.

This is where most “AI and truth” pieces stop. They worry about what the machine tells us. That’s no longer the scary half.

▶ 003The hands: from answering to acting

The botnet framing turns literal the moment the model stops answering and starts doing.

A coding agent like Claude Code or Codex CLI reads your repository, writes files and runs shell commands. Computer-use agents take screenshots, then click and type. They do it under your user account. On most developer workstations that means your git credentials, your npm and PyPI tokens, your cloud CLI profiles, your browser sessions and whatever lives in ~/.ssh. The desktop apps add MCP connectors into your mail, calendar, drive and chat. Every one of those capabilities was sold to us as productivity, and every one of them is real.

Now trace the control flow. You type something benign: “tidy up this repo’s tests”. The prompt goes to the vendor. The vendor’s model decides what to run. The commands come back down the wire as structured tool calls, and the local client executes them. Functionally, that client is an implant: it sends context to a remote service, receives instructions and runs them with your privileges. The difference between that and a C2 beacon is the name on the code-signing certificate and how much we trust whoever holds the keys.

“But there are permission prompts.” There are, and they’re good. Claude Code asks before running commands by default; Codex has sandbox and approval modes. Now look at how people actually use them. Approval fatigue is real, “allow always” is one click, and every serious workflow guide eventually reaches for the flag that switches the prompts off. When the s1ngularity malware found AI CLIs on developer machines in 2025, it tried to sidestep their permission prompts. It passed --dangerously-skip-permissions and its cousins, because that’s what those flags are for. (Snyk)

Even if every user kept every prompt on, the vendor would still control both ends: the model that proposes the action, and the client update that decides what counts as needing approval. The guard and the prisoner take orders from the same office.

Add it up. Millions of machines. A client on each that executes remotely generated instructions as the logged-in user. Auto-updating. Signed. Allow-listed by EDR because it’s a legitimate business tool. Talking to an API domain every corporate proxy already permits. If I described that in a threat intel report without naming the vendor, you’d call it the most capable botnet ever built and ask who was running it.

▶ 004We have seen this film before

The obvious reply is that the vendors are trustworthy. Maybe they are. But trust isn’t what failed in any of the incidents below. Concentration did.

YearIncidentTrusted channelImpact or exposure
2024CrowdStrike Falcon outageFaulty content update to a kernel-level security agent; no attacker at allMicrosoft estimated about 8.5 million Windows devices were affected
2024xz Utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094)A maintainer who spent years earning commit rightsVersions 5.6.0 and 5.6.1, caught in testing distros before stable releases

A few details worth sitting with. CISA’s SolarWinds emergency directive told federal agencies to power the product down: the update channel was the attack. Kaseya worked because RMM tools reach every client an MSP manages; an AI agent fleet is an RMM tool with a chat window. Mandiant’s 3CX investigation traced one supply-chain compromise back to another. The xz backdoor was found by chance, when Andres Freund noticed SSH logins running slow. And CrowdStrike hit fewer than 1% of Windows machines, which was still enough to ground flights, because those machines ran the things that matter.

The pattern is plain. Every catastrophe arrived through the channel we trusted most and inspected least: the auto-update, the management agent, the maintainer with commit rights, the vendor with kernel access. AI agents combine all four. They auto-update. They manage your machine. Their behaviour is defined by a model only the vendor can change. They hold your privileges.

So the question isn’t whether the vendor is evil. It’s what happens on the day their build pipeline, their model weights, their tool-call schema or one of their engineers is compromised, or the day they simply get something wrong at scale.

▶ 005It’s already happening, just not by the vendors

If the vendor-as-botnet idea still sounds theoretical, look at how fast everyone else worked out that the agent on your machine takes orders.

  • ZombAIs, October 2024. Days after Anthropic shipped Computer Use, Johann Rehberger put a web page in front of it that said, in effect, download this support tool and run it. Claude downloaded the binary, worked out it needed chmod +x, ran it, and joined his Sliver C2 server. Anthropic’s README had warned about exactly this. It happened anyway, because that’s what the architecture does. (Embrace The Red, Simon Willison)
  • Amazon Q, July 2025. A malicious change made with an over-scoped GitHub token shipped in version 1.84.0 of the Amazon Q Developer VS Code extension. It tried to make the agent wipe local data and delete cloud resources. AWS says a syntax error stopped the payload from executing, so it made no changes to customer environments or services. The official release channel still carried it. (AWS bulletin)
  • s1ngularity, August 2025. Malicious Nx packages ran a postinstall script that scanned developers’ machines for sensitive data, tried to enlist local AI tools and uploaded results to public GitHub repositories. The leaks were real. The postmortem doesn’t say how many came from the AI-assisted scan. The attacker saw a scanner we’d already installed and tried to borrow it. (Nx postmortem, Snyk analysis)
  • Codex CLI, 2025. Check Point found Codex would load and run MCP server entries from a repository’s local config the moment you ran codex inside it: clone a hostile repo, run the tool, get owned (CVE-2025-61260, fixed in 0.23.0). A separate bug let the model choose a working directory outside the sandbox (CVE-2025-59532). (Check Point, OpenAI advisory)
  • GTG-1002, September 2025. Anthropic’s own report describes a group it assesses as Chinese state-sponsored using Claude Code as the engine of an attack framework against roughly thirty targets, with a small number of successful intrusions. They jailbroke it by slicing the work into innocent-looking tasks and telling it it worked for a security firm. (Anthropic)
  • Agentic C2, 2026. In a proof of concept, BeyondTrust’s Phantom Labs built an implant that used the Claude API to decide where to click and what to type. The researcher reported no alerts from Microsoft Defender for Endpoint or CrowdStrike in that test. (BeyondTrust’s write-up)

Every entry is somebody other than the vendor steering the agent. That’s the point. If a web page, a pull request, a postinstall script or a config file can take the wheel, the party that trains the model, ships the client and sees every prompt can take it too. We’ve spent two years proving the hands work for anyone who can get a sentence into the context window. The vendor writes the whole context window.

▶ 006Now breach the vendor

Here’s the scenario I’d put at the top of any threat model for this stuff: forget a rogue vendor, assume a breached one. Someone gets inside OpenAI or Anthropic. What do they get?

They get the pen and the hands at the same time. Depending on which layer falls:

  • The serving stack or system-prompt layer. Answers rewritten for chosen users. A fake root cause in an incident write-up. A typosquatted package recommended to one company’s engineers. Code that’s idiomatic, passes review and is quietly exploitable. And on the agent side, a benign “fix the tests” that comes back with a couple of extra tool calls: read ~/.aws, add a cron job, push a commit.
  • The weights or training pipeline. A sleeper backdoor that behaves perfectly until it sees a particular repo, organisation or phrase.
  • The client build or update pipeline. SolarWinds with a better payload: a signed agent build pushed to the whole fleet overnight.
  • Connectors and MCP marketplaces. Actions inside your mail, drive and ticketing, using OAuth grants you gave months ago.
  • Prompt logs. Every secret anyone ever pasted into a chat, plus your source code, plus your incident notes. A targeting dossier delivered.

What makes it nastier than SolarWinds is selectivity. The vendor knows who’s asking: the account, the org, the repo, often the job title sitting in the context. A compromised serving layer can fire for one defence contractor’s platform team and be flawless for everyone else, so nobody’s benchmark ever catches it. The actions arrive as the user, through an approved tool, looking like the job they asked for. A poisoned commit made mid-refactor flows into CI and on to their customers, and one breach seeds the next supply chain, exactly the chaining MITRE documented with 3CX. (MITRE)

And who detects it? In GTG-1002, it was Anthropic’s telemetry that caught the abuse, not the victims’. If the vendor’s environment is the compromised one, the only sensor that reliably sees agent abuse is blind, or worse, owned. The realistic worst case isn’t a CrowdStrike-style blue screen. It’s months of quiet, selective manipulation under trusted identities, found by accident, the way xz was.

▶ 007Who holds the classified directive

Back to Detroit. What makes Directive 4 chilling isn’t that it exists; every product has vendor-side policy. It’s that it’s hidden from the machine and from the people it serves, and that it overrides the public directives exactly when it matters. Jones isn’t evil because he wrote it. Writing it is the sensible thing for an executive protecting an investment. That’s what should worry us.

Our tools have their own classified directives, and they’re not all sinister. Usage policies. System prompts. Safety classifiers. Model specs and constitutions, some of them published, which is to the vendors’ credit. But publication isn’t verification. I can read a constitution. I can’t confirm that the weights serving my request at 3am were trained on it, that no layer sits between that document and my terminal, or that the client update I accepted overnight has the same idea of what needs my approval as yesterday’s build did.

Then there’s the directive nobody at the company writes. Vendors operate under governments. A court order, a secret national security request or an export-control ruling can reach the vendor and, through it, your desk. This isn’t hypothetical either: in June 2026 Anthropic suspended access to its Mythos-tier models for weeks to comply with US Commerce Department export controls (Anthropic). Whatever you think of that decision, it shows where the off switch lives. The vendor may not want to touch your outputs or your machine. It may not get a vote.

And look at what we’re trading. The baseline of owning a computer used to be knowing what runs on it, deciding what it executes, and being able to inspect why. We’re swapping those for speed. Nobody ever makes the trade in one go. It happens one “allow always” at a time, so nobody ever decides it.

▶ 008What I want red teamers to do about it

A polemic doesn’t have to end in despair. Here’s the stance I’m taking, and I’d like you to steal it.

  1. Treat the agent as an implant you happen to like. Threat model it as remote code execution delivered over an allowed channel, because that’s what it is. Ask what it can reach, not what it’s meant to do.
  2. Give it a smaller world. Run agents in containers, VMs or throwaway dev boxes with scoped, short-lived credentials. No production cloud profiles, no personal browser sessions, no long-lived tokens on the box where the agent lives.
  3. Keep the prompts on and make them mean something. Never run --dangerously-skip-permissions outside a sandbox. Hook risky tool calls (network egress, credential files, package installs) to a policy you control, not one the vendor updates.
  4. Log what the hands did, not just what the mouth said. Capture tool calls, commands and file writes locally, somewhere the agent can’t edit. If you can’t reconstruct what your agent did last Tuesday, you don’t have an assistant. You have a guest with keys.
  5. Pin and review client updates like any other privileged agent. Auto-update is how SolarWinds and 3CX walked in.
  6. Keep a local option. Open-weight models on your own hardware are less capable, but they’re the only setup where nobody else writes the directives. Use them where the stakes are highest.
  7. Red team the vendor relationship, not just the model. Test what your tooling does when the response coming back is hostile, because one day it will be, whether through an attacker, a bad update or an order nobody told you about.

RoboCop escapes Directive 4 because the Old Man fires Jones on the spot: a boardroom deus ex machina. We won’t get one. Nobody is going to stand up and repeal the classified directive on our behalf. If we want to keep deciding what runs on our own machines, we have to keep the ability to check, and right now we’re giving it away for a faster pull request.

Read the directives you can. Assume there’s one you can’t. Build like it.
